## What they did

A **systematic review** (PRISMA + PROSPERO-registered, SIGN Methodology Checklist 4 for risk of bias) of game-based cognitive assessments (GBCAs — the paper's umbrella term for both "gamified" traditional tasks and purpose-built "serious games") that have been **both psychometrically validated and user-evaluated** in people aged **≥50**. Databases: PsycINFO, Embase, IEEE, MEDLINE, searched 2022-12-09, published-2015-onward. **15,232 records → 7,595 after de-duplication → 649 to abstract screening → 83 to full-text → 8 studies included.** No meta-analysis was possible (too heterogeneous); the paper is a narrative synthesis across two themes — **system usability** and **subjective user experience** — of those 8 games, total combined n across all 8 ≈ 502 participants (with overlapping control/patient subgroups per study, see table below). Not itself a study of any one product — it's the evidence map behind "do older people, and older people with cognitive impairment specifically, find these games usable and fun."

## The three findings that matter to MindMitra

1. **Purpose-built simplicity does not guarantee usability for people with cognitive impairment — and the paper says this is true even for the easiest, most literally-designed games.** Direct quote: *"in several of the game studies, older adults and those with cognitive impairment tended to find GBCAs less usable. This trend was observed even when the games were explicitly designed for these populations, and the tasks were simplistic and representative of basic daily activities."* In two of the four SUS-scored studies (Numberlink, search-and-match task/SMT), usability fell significantly with age/impairment (SMT: 88.67→79.09→68.25 across young→older→oldest, P=.01). In the GBCA study (Wang et al 2022), lower user-experience scores tracked with **higher clinical dementia rating, older age, and lower education** — despite the interface being deliberately built with large buttons, pictures and spoken instructions. This is the single most important caution for our entire product: good intentions and elder-friendly design principles are not sufficient; usability for people who already have cognitive impairment must be tested with that population specifically, not assumed from general elderly-UX guidelines.

2. **Difficulty band, independently derived from a different literature than ENHANCE, with the same shape.** *"the importance of perceived challenge in mediating gameplay experience across groups. That is, generating the appropriate level of difficulty for each user is important for positive user experiences, specifically enjoyment."* Games with more difficulty levels (SMT: 71 possible levels; Numberlink) held enjoyment/motivation flat across young/older/oldest groups even as perceived *challenge* varied significantly — while games with a single fixed difficulty (Kitchen and Cooking) saw satisfaction split sharply by impairment level in the *opposite* direction from other studies. Their explicit design lesson: *"Providing Each User With Their Optimal Challenge Is Key… this personalization does not need to be highly sophisticated to be useful."* This corroborates (independently, different games, different country, different methodology) the ENHANCE paper's adaptive-difficulty finding — two unrelated evidence bases now support our RL-driven difficulty engine as the correct design response, not just a nice-to-have.

3. **A GBCA's whole point is to produce failure, which is structurally in tension with "make it feel good" — the paper names this explicitly and we have never said it out loud.** *"intentionally producing user failure states is a central design tenet when developing GBCAs, and this... may cause reductions in usability and subjective user experiences."* And: *"if the primary goal of a cognitive test is the detection of cognitive deficits, the implication is that people with cognitive impairment will likely (1) fail to achieve certain tasks, (2) take longer to complete objectives, and (3) make more errors than cognitively healthy people."* This is a direct challenge to any slide that says our games are simultaneously (a) an accurate cognitive-tracking signal for the caregiver dashboard and (b) a purely positive, failure-free, always-encouraging experience for the elder. The paper's resolution — "good enough" usability, not maximal — is a framing we should adopt explicitly rather than let a judge catch the tension for us.

## One number I can put on a slide

**8 studies survived screening out of 15,232 initial records (0.05%) — this is a young, thin evidence base, not an established field.** Measures how much rigorously validated *and* user-evaluated GBCA research exists for older/cognitively-impaired users as of the search date (Dec 2022). Caveat: search cut-off is now ~4 years old and the field moves fast; also earlier reviews cited in this paper found "user experience evaluation was performed in <25% of interventions, with usability and enjoyability assessed in only 13% and 18%, respectively" [ref 19] — so the 8/15,232 figure is a floor, but the general thinness is real and worth stating plainly: nobody has a large evidence base here, including us.

## What this changes about our product

- **Test with people who actually have cognitive impairment, not just "elderly" testers.** The review's core warning is that impairment-specific usability failures appeared even in games explicitly designed for this population. Our accessibility/usability testing plan (whatever it becomes post-hackathon) must include participants with diagnosed MCI/dementia, not only cognitively healthy elders — general elder-UX heuristics (large buttons, high contrast, simple language) are necessary but the paper shows they are not sufficient.
- **Name the failure-state tension on our own terms, in the deck.** State explicitly: our games are calibrated to be "fun enough" and encouraging while still producing an honest difficulty signal for the caregiver-facing analytics — cite this paper's own resolution ("good enough usability and user experience are good enough" for a short, sporadic screener) rather than claim zero-failure, maximally-fun and clinically-meaningful-signal simultaneously, which this paper shows is not coherent.
- **Prefer touchscreen/tablet over mouse, and validate this is not itself an accessibility barrier.** Three separate included studies (SASG, Episodix, GBCA) independently found or designed for touch-over-mouse preference; but note the counter-finding from outside this review's own discussion — Tong et al. [ref 18, cited in their Introduction] found ergonomic difficulty holding a touch device was itself a barrier. Both are true; test grip/dexterity requirements, don't assume touch solves usability.
- **Decide, per game, whether it is self-administered or coach/ASHA-administered — do not assume self-administration is neutral.** Two studies found the presence of a health professional was "essential to the success" of the intervention (Episodix, Kitchen and Cooking): users trusted the assessment more, and clinicians helped explain the game to patients *and family*, tracked progress, and adapted to changing needs. Meanwhile self-administered designs (VST) intentionally withheld support to test unassisted usability — and found a significant correlation between lower usability ratings and longer completion time. Our own product mixes an offline self-play mode with an ASHA-supported onboarding — this paper is direct support for keeping the ASHA layer involved in *interpreting* results, not just deploying the tablet, and it pairs with the ENHANCE finding that coaching is essential.
- **Watch the "video game" framing.** Two independent qualitative sub-findings: Episodix participants "preferred the digital intervention to be referred to as a test... because they perceived the term video game to have negative and nonserious connotations," and the authors' own prison-population study found GBCAs with immersive game features "risked being perceived as too childish for the serious context of a cognitive test." Avoid over-gamified framing (mascots, cartoonish rewards) for anything positioned as an assessment; reserve playful framing for the pure engagement/stimulation activities, and consider language choices carefully in NER-language localisation, where "game" vs "exercise" vs "test" will carry different connotations per language/culture — flag this for [../15-ner-cultural-adaptation.md](../15-ner-cultural-adaptation.md).
- **Multiple difficulty levels / procedurally varied rounds, independent of our RL layer, has its own direct justification here:** more difficulty levels → flatter enjoyment/motivation across ability groups, and randomised round-to-round variation "can also make it possible to detect more subtle impairments and reduce learning effects" [SASG finding cited via Zygouris et al]. This is a second, independent reason (alongside the adaptive engine) to avoid a small fixed set of difficulty tiers — build a continuous or near-continuous difficulty space, not 3 discrete levels.
- **Nothing changes** about our reminders, offline sync, or multilingual/voice plans — this review contains no evidence on any of those.

## What this forbids us from claiming

- **This is not evidence that any specific game design works for dementia patients — it is evidence that *evaluating* GBCAs on this population is rare and results are mixed.** Do not cite "systematic review shows game-based cognitive assessment works for older adults with cognitive impairment." The correct sentence is closer to: *"a systematic review of validated game-based cognitive assessments found usability and user experience results were mixed and often worse for older/more impaired users, even in games purpose-built for them."*
- **These are assessment/screening games, not therapeutic or training games.** Explicit exclusion criterion: *"Training or rehabilitation focused without assessment"* was excluded. Every one of the 8 studies is about *detecting* cognitive impairment (screening/diagnosis-adjacent), not about *improving* cognition. We cannot use this paper to support any claim that our memory-training or attention games improve cognitive outcomes — that evidence must come from Woods et al. Cochrane 2023 (item #1) instead. This also means: **we should not misquote this paper as supporting our therapeutic/training use case at all** — its entire object of study is assessment games, which is closer to our future clinical-screening ambitions than to our current cognitive-stimulation-game core.
- **No meta-analytic effect size exists — do not imply a pooled number.** The authors explicitly say heterogeneity "prevented a meta-analysis." Every figure in this paper is study-specific and must be cited per-study (e.g., "in the SMT study, SUS fell from 88.67 to 68.25 across age groups"), never blended into a single "GBCAs score X on usability" statistic.
- **All 8 games were research prototypes, not products.** *"most of the games did not seem to be publicly available"* and all games were researcher-built (bar SASG, built by a separate academic group) — **none were commercial games**. We cannot say "commercially available cognitive games have been validated as usable" — that claim is untested by this review.
- **Sample sizes per study are small — do not imply population-level certainty.** Total study samples ranged n=16 (Episodix) to n=139 (SASG); several impairment subgroups were single digits to low double digits (e.g., Episodix: MCI n=3, AD n=5). Report per-study n whenever citing a per-study result.
- **We cannot claim this validates difficulty adaptation as clinically effective — only as preferred.** The difficulty-band finding is a *user-experience/enjoyment* result (self-report), not a cognitive-outcome or diagnostic-accuracy result. Method evidence for how to actually implement adaptive difficulty must still come from the DDA review (item #9), not this paper.
- **No data on rural, low-resource, non-Western, or non-English-speaking populations.** All 8 studies are from high-income-country research settings (see country table below); nothing here speaks to NER conditions, connectivity, or Indic languages.
- **Do not present the "self-administration has downsides" finding as settled.** It comes from qualitative/secondary interpretation in 2 of 8 studies (Episodix, Kitchen and Cooking), against a different design intent in another (VST, which intentionally tested unsupported self-administration and does not report the same finding). Phrase as "some evidence, from a minority of included studies" — not as a general rule.

## Quality check

**Peer-reviewed?** Yes — *JMIR Aging*, peer-reviewed (editor: Rumei Yang; reviewed by Maximillian Friehs, Nuria Vita-Barrull, Adi Katz), published 2025-06-11. Registered prospectively on PROSPERO (CRD42023433298) before the search was run — a real methodological strength; protocol deviations, if any, aren't flagged. Followed PRISMA reporting and a named risk-of-bias tool (SIGN Methodology Checklist 4 for case-control studies, chosen because most included games were still pilot-stage). Two independent reviewers at abstract and full-text screening stages, with disagreements resolved by 3-way vote (12/83 full-text papers, 15%, needed this — a normal and disclosed rate).
**Sample size?** The review itself: 8 studies. Underlying participant totals: SASG n=139, SMT n=52, Kitchen and Cooking n=21, Numberlink n=55 (incl. PD n=4, HD n=5), Episodix n=16, Vallejo virtual games n=38, Wang GBCA n=124, VST n=57. All small-to-modest by clinical-trial standards; typical for early-stage usability/validation pilots, not adequate for generalizable prevalence or effect claims.
**Population like ours or not?** *Closer to our clinical target than ENHANCE, but not NER.* These studies include actual people with **MCI, Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's, Huntington's, and subjective cognitive decline** — unlike ENHANCE, this is our actual patient population by diagnosis. But: all 8 studies are set in high-income countries with presumably high literacy, standard research-lab or clinic conditions, and none are from South/Southeast Asia. Country/origin isn't tabulated explicitly in the text extracted here, but author affiliations and the underlying game names (SASG=Italy, Numberlink/SMT=likely UK/Europe research groups, VST=Greece, Episodix=Spain, Kitchen and Cooking=France/Europe, Wang GBCA=likely China, Vallejo=Spain) suggest Europe/East-Asia concentration, not South Asia or NER. Treat clinical-population relevance as high, cultural/setting relevance as low.
**Conflicts of interest?** One disclosed: *"MK is the chief executive officer of Arludo"* — Arludo Holdings Pty Ltd is listed as author Michael Kasumovic's affiliation (a games/citizen-science company), and Kasumovic is a co-author on this review, not just cited as a study author. This is a real conflict to flag: one review author has a commercial interest in serious-games technology generally, though Arludo does not appear to be the developer of any of the 8 included games (all researcher-built, one — SASG — by a separate named group). Funded by an Australian NHMRC Ideas Grant (GA143456, "ASCAPE": screening for cognition in older prisoners) — a government research grant, no industry funding disclosed for the review itself.
**Would I defend citing this to a hostile judge?** **Y** — for the core "usability is not guaranteed by good intentions, even for purpose-built impairment-specific games" finding, for the difficulty-band/perceived-challenge finding, and for the self-administration-vs-clinician-presence tension — all are the review's own stated synthesis conclusions, well-sourced to specific studies, and the review's methodology (PRISMA, PROSPERO, dual screening) is solid. **N** — for any claim about therapeutic/training efficacy (out of scope by design), any pooled/average usability number (no meta-analysis was possible), any claim about non-Western or rural populations, or any claim that a *specific* one of the 8 games is validated for our use — none are publicly available or usable by us directly.

## Search strategy and PRISMA funnel

- Databases: **PsycINFO, Embase, IEEE, MEDLINE**. Search terms (Table 1), broad Boolean: (`game* OR gami* OR virtual reality OR VR`) AND (`cogn* OR MCI OR dementia OR neurocog* OR neuropsych*`) AND (`assess* OR test* OR measur* OR collect* OR detect*`).
- Search run **2022-12-09**. **15,232** records → **7,595** after de-duplication (Covidence) → **6,946** excluded at title screening → **649** to abstract screening (2 reviewers, RM + MD; disagreements resolved by YIJH 3-way vote) → **83** to full-text screening → **12/83 (15%)** needed consensus voting → **8 studies** included for extraction.
- Quality appraisal: SIGN "Methodology Checklist 4: Case Control Studies" (chosen because most games were pilot-stage, not yet ready for more sophisticated diagnostic-accuracy appraisal).

**Inclusion criteria (Textbox 1):** user-evaluated; people aged ≥50; psychometrically validated; assessment or screening (not training/rehab-only); cognitive functioning domain; primary research; **2015 onward**; digital serious games or gamified tasks.
**Exclusion criteria:** did not include target population/outcome; high-immersion (headset-based) interventions; training/rehab-focused without assessment; no psychometric validation; no user evaluation; no game-performance data collected; inappropriate design (reviews, conference abstracts); social-cognition-only focus.
**Definitional choices worth remembering:** "older people" defined as **≥50 years** (not 60/65), deliberately inclusive of marginalised groups (people experiencing homelessness or incarceration) who show age-related cognitive risk earlier. Validation could have occurred in an earlier study of the same game — not necessarily within the included paper itself. 4 of the 8 included studies self-described as "virtual reality" but were **low-immersion**, playable on tablet/computer via touch or mouse — the review's definition of "GBCA" absorbed these under the digital/low-immersion umbrella rather than excluding them, since true headset/high-immersion VR was excluded.

## Usability findings (system-level), by study

- **5 of 8 studies** reported system-level usability results (SASG, VST, Numberlink, SMT, Vallejo virtual games), all via self-report.
- **SASG**: no formal usability score — participants asked about familiarity with computers/touchscreens; familiarity **did not** affect game performance score. Authors attribute this to the simple first-person design (watering flowers with radio on, dialling a phone number) and to choosing **touch screen over mouse**.
- **SUS (System Usability Scale)** used in the remaining 4: 10-item, 5-point Likert, 0–100 aggregate. Reference bands cited: 50th percentile ≈ **68**; good = **71–84**; excellent ≥ **85**.
  - **VST**: mean SUS **83.11 (SD 14.6)**. No significant difference between SCD and MCI groups. Significant correlation between SUS score and average completion time (**r = −0.496, P<.001**) — lower usability rating associated with longer completion time, direction of causality unclear per authors' own caveat (quoted above). VST was **intentionally unsupported self-administration** — examiner set up the tablet and launched the game but gave no help thereafter, by design, to test unassisted usability.
  - **Numberlink**: SUS **93.38 (SD 5.72)** young · **93.33 (SD 6.25)** older · **83.39 (SD 11.79)** oldest · **81.88 (SD 9.66)** Parkinson's · **83.12 (SD 10.68)** Huntington's. Significant group difference, **P=.02**.
  - **SMT**: SUS **88.67 (SD 7.28)** younger · **79.09 (SD 15.50)** older · **68.25 (SD 18.78)** oldest. Significant age-group effect, **P=.01** — usability *decreasing* with age.
  - **Vallejo virtual games**: SUS **83.5 (SD 11.16)** controls · **83.75 (SD 9.82)** AD. **No significant group difference**, and no significant difference by touch-device familiarity either.

## User experience findings, by study

- **5 of 8 studies** examined user experience (Episodix, Numberlink, SMT, Kitchen and Cooking, Wang GBCA).
- **Episodix**: pre/post questionnaire on usefulness + motivation. Motivation to play video games **increased 40%** after gameplay (from a low pre-play baseline). Episodix rated more engaging than the CVLT pen-and-paper test it was based on. Design process included focus groups + pilot, which is credited for the touch-over-mouse preference finding (mirrors SASG). Qualitative: perceived as "very useful," but participants **preferred clinician administration** over self-administration regardless of their own confidence; preferred the intervention be called a **"test," not a "video game"** (negative/nonserious connotation of "game").
- **Numberlink & SMT** (both via the **Perception of Game Training Questionnaire**, ref 50), rated enjoyment/challenge/frustration/motivation:
  - Numberlink: enjoyment P=.43, challenge P=.07, frustration P=.06, motivation P=.67 — **none significant** between groups; slight (nonsignificant) trend toward more challenge/frustration in older/impaired participants, especially PD and HD. Authors: further difficulty-adaptation "might partially reverse this trend."
  - SMT: significant difference in challenge ratings across the 3 age groups (**P<.001**); young rated it significantly less challenging than older (**P=.02**); significant age-group differences in difficulty ratings for both short (**P<.001**) and long (**P<.001**) versions. **But no significant group differences in enjoyment, frustration, or motivation** — attributed to the game's **71 possible difficulty levels** allowing responsiveness to the player.
- **Wang GBCA**: control group very satisfied; **NCD (neurocognitive disorder) group significantly lower** across the board. Lower scores correlated with **higher clinical dementia rating, older age, lower education**. This despite an interface deliberately built for older users: large pictures/buttons, spoken instructions, tap/drag/speech-input interaction modes (low-cost speech recognition).
- **Kitchen and Cooking**: satisfaction/interest/motivation (intrinsic + extrinsic)/emotional experience/fatigue measured twice. High satisfaction, interest, motivation overall; **intrinsic motivation significantly higher than extrinsic**; scores stable across the two sessions (no novelty decay, but also no upward trend as seen in Episodix). **AD participants significantly more satisfied than MCI participants (P=.04)** — the *inverse* of the pattern in Numberlink/SMT/Wang GBCA, where healthier = more satisfied. Authors credit **clinician presence** as critical: explaining the game to patients and families, tracking progress, adapting to changing needs, maintaining motivation.

## The theoretical framing worth reusing directly

- **Usability vs. user experience, as the paper defines them** (from Hassenzahl & Tractinsky, ISO 9241-210:2019): *usability* = whether a system can be used by its intended users to achieve goals "with effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction in a specified context of use" — pragmatic, technical (interface, ergonomics, bugs). *User experience* = the subjective, hedonic layer — "users' emotions, beliefs, preferences, perceptions, comfort, behaviors, and accomplishments that occur before, during and after use." Good usability makes something easy to use; good UX makes it feel good to use. Useful vocabulary for our own UX documentation.
- **Self-determination theory framing for difficulty**, cited from the broader literature (their refs 61–63): a player experiences **low competence** (and reduced enjoyment/motivation) when challenge is too great relative to ability; the "optimal challenge / sweet spot" is mediated by the individual's own difficulty threshold. This is the theoretical backing behind both the difficulty-band finding here and the ENHANCE quote about not raising difficulty right after a miss.
- **The floor/ceiling tension, stated precisely**: making a GBCA easier (to be usable/enjoyable for impaired users) trades off against the game's *sensitivity as an assessment instrument* — "a game that is too simple or too domain specific may not be relevant for healthier older adults or those with milder... impairment, even if it is more appropriate for people with more significant cognitive decline." Their resolution: **more difficulty levels widen both the floor and the ceiling simultaneously**, partially resolving (not eliminating) the tension — this is a direct, reusable design argument for our adaptive engine's dynamic range, distinct from the reward-shaping argument.
- **Design preference literature for older users, secondhand via the review's own prior work and cited sources**: prefer intellectually stimulating games (puzzle, educational, strategy) over reflex-based; prefer single-player; prefer real-life-task replication or puzzle mechanics over fantasy/violent content; avoid multiplayer/competitive; avoid "childish" framing (found condescending in the authors' own prison-population qualitative study, ref 25); large touchscreen tablets generally more usable/intuitive than mouse or small devices, **but** can create ergonomic difficulty for frail users or those with dyspraxia/visuospatial impairment (citing Tong et al., ref 18, on a whack-a-mole go/no-go game where holding the touch device itself was the barrier).
- **The paper's own named prior findings that motivated this review**: Birk et al. [ref 17] — adding game elements to standard cognitive tasks (go/no-go, n-back) did **not** improve engagement vs. the original task, and gamification actually **reduced performance** on the go/no-go task. A prior systematic review of game-based neuropsych interventions [ref 19] found user-experience evaluation was performed in **<25%** of interventions, usability in **13%**, enjoyability in **18%** — the evidentiary gap this review is filling.

## The 4 "implications for practice," as printed

1. **Validity is necessary but not sufficient.** A game that is psychometrically valid but not usable/engaging "is unlikely to work in practice." Co-design with users and domain experts throughout, not just validation at the end.
2. **Clarify the intended user.** Simple gamified realistic tasks suit more-impaired older users better; complex serious games suit younger/healthier users better and lose appeal with age/impairment. "Good enough" usability/UX is probably sufficient for a short, sporadic screener targeting at-risk older adults — a different bar than an engagement-maximising product for a broader/younger audience.
3. **Design for older people's actual preferences.** Avoid childlike features, favor intellectual/puzzle framing and real-life-task replication, avoid fantasy/violence/multiplayer. Tablets generally beat mouse/VR for usability — with the ergonomic caveat above.
4. **Provide each user their optimal challenge wherever possible** — difficulty balancing need not be sophisticated to help; more levels / randomised round variation both raises sensitivity to subtle impairment and reduces learning effects.

## Limitations, in the authors' own words

Small samples throughout; different target subgroups per study (impairment type, age band) prevent clean comparison. Most studies treated user evaluation as a **secondary** outcome to psychometric validity, which likely limits evaluation rigor. All 8 games were distinct, bespoke, and mostly **not publicly available**, "creating uncertainty about how well developed and sophisticated the games actually are in practice" and blocking deeper technical/mechanics review. All user-evaluation data were self-report, with the associated well-known biases. SUS, while validated generally, is "short and generic" and **its validity for people with neurocognitive disorders has not been established** — a direct caveat on several of the headline SUS numbers above. Other UX instruments were disparate/customised, preventing quantitative synthesis or meta-analysis. The SIGN case-control checklist was used because most games were still pilot-stage, not ready for more rigorous diagnostic-accuracy appraisal — implying the underlying evidence quality ceiling is itself modest.

# Against our problem statement (PS: AI-based cognitive gaming and memory assistance for elderly dementia patients, NER)

| PS clause | What this paper gives us | What it does not |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) Cognitive games** — memory, attention, routine recall, pattern/object recognition | Direct clinical-population evidence (MCI, AD, PD, HD, SCD) that impairment-specific usability/UX failures happen even in purpose-built simple games — the strongest caution in our whole evidence base against assuming our games will "just work" for dementia patients without testing on that exact population. Also: intellectual/puzzle framing, real-life-task replication, and avoiding childish/fantasy content are all supported design preferences for our game catalogue. | No therapeutic/training evidence (explicitly excluded by design) — cannot support memory-*improvement* claims, only assessment/engagement design choices. |
| **(b) AI/ML adaptive difficulty (Q-learning RL)** | A second, independently-derived evidentiary pillar (alongside ENHANCE) for adaptive difficulty: more difficulty levels/procedural variation flattens enjoyment/motivation gaps across ability groups and widens the assessment's floor/ceiling simultaneously. Self-determination theory citation gives us academic grounding beyond just user quotes. | No RL-specific evidence, no test of *how* to adapt, no outcome data on whether adaptation actually improves detection accuracy or engagement — those remain open questions for the DDA review (item #9). |
| **(c) Multilingual + voice** | Nothing directly. One tangential point: the Wang GBCA game included **speech-input** as an interaction mode (via low-cost speech recognition) alongside tap/drag, precedent that voice-as-input is workable in a GBCA context. | No language, dialect, or ASR/TTS evidence of any kind. |
| **(d) Engagement — visuals, accessibility** | Concrete, sourced design preferences: touch over mouse (with the ergonomic caveat), large buttons/pictures/spoken instructions as an *attempted* solution that still underperformed for higher-impairment users in one study (Wang GBCA) — i.e., a caution that these standard accommodations are necessary but not sufficient. Avoid framing content as "childish" or as a "video game" if it's meant to feel legitimate/serious to the user. | No specific findings on colour, contrast, motion, or flashing content (unlike ENHANCE) — this review's accessibility content is about interaction modality and framing, not visual design specifics. |
| **(e) Reminders** | Nothing. Out of scope for this review. | — |
| **(f) Caregiver / health-worker dashboards** | Two studies' findings support keeping a human (clinician/ASHA) in the loop for administering and interpreting results, not just deploying the tablet — clinician presence explained the game to patients *and families*, tracked progress over time, and adapted to changing needs (Kitchen and Cooking); users trusted clinician-administered assessment more than self-administered even when personally confident (Episodix). | No dashboard usability data, nothing about report templates or government formats. |
| **(g) Offline / low connectivity** | Nothing. Out of scope. | — |
| **(h) Mobile/tablet, elderly-friendly UI** | Tablet-over-mouse preference corroborated across multiple independent studies (SASG, Episodix), reinforcing our tablet-first platform choice — with the caveat that tablets can be ergonomically hard for frail/dyspraxic users, which needs a design response (device stands, larger touch targets, alternative grips), not just a platform choice. | No data on rural connectivity, cost, or device-sharing scenarios relevant to NER households. |
| **Security / data** | Nothing. Out of scope. | — |

**Where this paper leaves our biggest gaps unfilled:** no NER, South Asian, or non-English-speaking population anywhere in the 8 studies; no therapeutic-efficacy evidence (by design, since it only reviews assessment games); no rural/offline/connectivity evidence. Its real contribution is a rigorous, citable warning against assuming purpose-built "elderly-friendly" design is sufficient for people with actual cognitive impairment — that warning belongs on our risks slide alongside the low-literacy gap already flagged in the ENHANCE summary ([03-enhance-app-usability.md](03-enhance-app-usability.md)).
